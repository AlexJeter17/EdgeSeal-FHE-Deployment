\section{Introduction}
In recent years, the role of health monitoring and remote data collection has grown tremendously, driven by advances in wearable devices, Internet of Things (IoT) technologies, and healthcare digitization. These systems provide physicians with real-time access to patient data, enabling earlier detection of health concerns, more accurate diagnoses, and improved patient outcomes~\cite{iqbal2021advances}. Continuous health monitoring has proven particularly valuable in chronic disease management, postoperative care, and emergency response~\cite{mohammed2019real}. By ensuring that clinicians have timely information, patients benefit from personalized treatment plans and a higher quality of care.

However, despite these benefits, health data security remains a pressing concern. As sensitive information travels from its source—such as wearable sensors, mobile health applications, or edge devices—to its destination in hospital systems, cloud servers, or electronic health records, it is exposed to numerous threats, including interception, manipulation, and unauthorized access~\cite{atadoga2024comparative}. Unlike other forms of personal information, health data is uniquely sensitive: its compromise can lead not only to financial or privacy harms but also to direct risks to patient safety.

The consequences of even minor manipulation of health data can be severe. For example, if vital signs such as heart rate or blood glucose levels are falsified or altered in transit, physicians may prescribe unnecessary treatments or overlook life-threatening conditions~\cite{10500025}. Similarly, delays caused by ransomware or denial-of-service attacks can prevent timely medical intervention in emergencies. These scenarios highlight that healthcare is not just another data-driven industry—errors or breaches here translate directly into health outcomes, sometimes with irreversible consequences~\cite{10500025,atadoga2024comparative}. Beyond the clinical risks, patients who lose confidence in the safety of digital health platforms may become reluctant to share their data or adopt new monitoring systems, limiting the reach and effectiveness of innovations designed to save lives. If clinical data is altered, even slightly, it can result in misdiagnosis, inappropriate treatments, or delayed interventions that put patients at serious risk~\cite{seh2020healthcare}. These risks extend beyond individual patients to systemic challenges, as data breaches can erode trust in digital health technologies, discourage patients from adopting monitoring systems, and slow down the integration of life-saving innovations into clinical practice. Moreover, regulators and healthcare organizations emphasize that safeguarding the integrity, confidentiality, and availability of health data is a non-negotiable requirement for advancing digital health~\cite{pandey2020key}. Without comprehensive protection, the full potential of health monitoring systems cannot be realized, making strong end-to-end security a fundamental prerequisite for healthcare innovation.

A variety of approaches have been developed to secure health data as it moves between systems. Encryption standards such as Transport Layer Security (TLS) are commonly applied to protect data during transmission, while symmetric algorithms like Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) are used for data at rest in storage~\cite{elhoseny2018secure}. Regulatory frameworks such as Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States further mandate strict controls to safeguard patient records, and emerging technologies such as blockchain have been proposed to strengthen data integrity and access control~\cite{luxton2012mhealth}. These measures represent important steps toward protecting patient information and building trust in digital healthcare systems.

Despite these efforts, most existing solutions provide only partial protection and leave critical gaps in the data life cycle~\cite{qiu2020secure,trader2025real}. Encryption is often applied at specific points, such as during transmission or storage, but not maintained consistently from the moment data leaves a patient's device until it is received and processed by a healthcare provider~\cite{elhoseny2018secure}. Intermediate nodes, such as cloud servers or third-party applications, may temporarily expose sensitive information, creating opportunities for interception or manipulation. Likewise, many wearable and IoT devices remain vulnerable due to limited computational resources, inconsistent patching, or reliance on insecure communication protocols. These shortcomings highlight that current solutions are not truly end-to-end, and therefore cannot guarantee continuous data integrity and confidentiality across the full path of healthcare data exchange~\cite{trader2025real}.

To address these challenges, we propose an \textbf{end-to-end privacy-preserving framework} that integrates Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) at the edge layer to secure healthcare data from IoT wearable devices. Health data is encrypted immediately after generation and remains protected through all transmission and processing stages, including analysis in the cloud. By embedding encryption and integrity verification mechanisms within the data flow, our framework eliminates exposure at intermediate nodes and ensures that healthcare providers receive reliable, tamper-proof data for clinical decision-making.

\noindent \textbf{Key Contributions of this Work:}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{End-to-End Privacy-Preserving Architecture:}
We design a secure IoT-to-cloud pipeline using the CKKS FHE scheme (via TenSEAL), ensuring that physiological data (ECG, BP, SpO₂) remains encrypted from acquisition to analysis, eliminating all plaintext exposure.

\item \textbf{Threat Model and Security Guarantees:}
Our framework resists both passive interception and active tampering by embedding encryption and verification within each data exchange stage, maintaining confidentiality and integrity even under adversarial network conditions.

\item \textbf{Enhancing Diagnostic Reliability:}
By securing and validating data at its source, the proposed system delivers tamper-proof medical information, preventing diagnostic errors and reinforcing clinician confidence in remote healthcare environments.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\section{System Model}

\subsection{Overview}
The proposed system enables \textbf{end-to-end privacy preservation} for healthcare data collected through IoT-based wearable devices. Health data is generated at the patient side, transmitted through nearby \textit{edge devices}, and securely processed on a \textit{cloud platform} for analysis. To prevent data manipulation or leakage at any point in this pipeline, the system integrates \textbf{Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE)} to ensure that sensitive data remains encrypted even during computation and analysis.

This model provides a secure communication and processing framework that maintains confidentiality, integrity, and availability across all stages of data transmission---from the \textit{source (wearable sensors)} to the \textit{destination (cloud analytics environment)} and back to the healthcare system.

\subsection{System Components}

\subsubsection{Wearable Data Receivers (IoT Devices)}
These are the primary data sources that continuously collect patient health metrics such as heart rate, temperature, oxygen saturation, or movement data. Each device transmits the raw sensor readings to a local \textit{edge node} via a secure communication protocol.

\subsubsection{Edge Devices}
Edge devices act as local gateways between the IoT sensors and the cloud platform. Before data leaves the local network, the edge node performs \textit{initial preprocessing} such as time-stamping, noise reduction, and format standardization. Most importantly, the edge layer is responsible for performing \textbf{FHE encryption}, converting plaintext medical data into encrypted ciphertext before transmission.

\subsubsection{FHE Encryption Module}
This module uses \textbf{Fully Homomorphic Encryption} to enable mathematical operations on encrypted data without decryption. This ensures that even if intercepted, the data cannot be read or modified by unauthorized entities. The encryption process occurs immediately after data preprocessing, meaning that no unencrypted data ever leaves the local edge network.

\subsubsection{IoT Cloud Platform}
The cloud layer receives and stores encrypted data from multiple edge devices. Using homomorphic operations, it can perform computations such as anomaly detection, risk prediction, or health trend analysis \textit{directly on encrypted data}. Since all analytics occur on ciphertext, the cloud never accesses or stores patient information in plaintext form.

\subsubsection{Secure and Privacy-Preserving Mechanisms for Data Analysis}
This layer contains privacy-preserving algorithms and analytics models that operate directly on FHE-encrypted data. These mechanisms ensure compliance with healthcare data regulations while maintaining system performance and usability. The proposed model serves as a foundation for future work on encrypted model inference and secure result decryption.

\subsection{Data Flow and Security Life cycle}
The secure data transmission and computation pipeline proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data Generation:} IoT wearable devices capture continuous health metrics.
    \item \textbf{Local Preprocessing:} Edge devices organize and prepare the data for encryption.
    \item \textbf{FHE Encryption:} The processed data are encrypted homomorphically at the edge device.
    \item \textbf{Encrypted Transmission:} The cipher text is securely transferred to the IoT cloud platform.
    \item \textbf{Encrypted Computation:} The cloud executes analytical or diagnostic tasks directly on encrypted data without exposure.
\end{enumerate}

This workflow guaranties that patient information is never exposed in plain text form, achieving continuous end-to-end protection and maintaining the integrity and confidentiality of transmitted health data.


\section{Proposed Solution}

\subsection{Design Goals}
The primary goal of this work is to develop an \textbf{end-to-end privacy-preserving framework} that ensures continuous security for healthcare data transmitted from IoT wearable devices. Although existing healthcare systems often secure data at discrete points, our approach integrates \textbf{Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE)} directly at the edge layer, providing continuous protection from the moment data are generated until they reach the healthcare provider or cloud platform. 

This design eliminates vulnerabilities introduced by intermediate network nodes, ensuring that data remain encrypted and unalterable throughout its entire life-cycle. The proposed framework is designed to achieve three major goals:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{End-to-End Security:} Guaranty that data remain encrypted from generation to analysis without exposure at any transmission or storage stage.
    \item \textbf{Privacy-Preserving Computation:} Enable mathematical operations on encrypted data without decryption using the CKKS homomorphic encryption scheme.
    \item \textbf{Efficient Edge Integration:} Deploy FHE encryption at the edge to balance computation cost and latency while maintaining high levels of data confidentiality.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Architecture Overview}
The proposed architecture is based on a layered model comprising IoT devices, edge nodes, and cloud infrastructure. Each layer contributes to data confidentiality, authenticity, and privacy through encryption and verification processes. Figure~\ref{fig:systemmodel} (see System Model section) illustrates the architecture.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IoT Devices:} Collect patient health data (e.g., ECG, blood pressure, temperature) and transmit it securely to the nearest edge node.
    \item \textbf{Edge Layer:} Acts as a gateway responsible for preprocessing and immediate FHE-based encryption of all data before it leaves the local network.
    \item \textbf{Cloud Layer:} Receives the encrypted data for storage and potential homomorphic analysis, ensuring that computation and aggregation can occur without decryption.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Homomorphic Encryption Workflow}
The system employs the \textbf{Cheon-Kim-Kim-Song (CKKS)} scheme, implemented through the \textit{TenSEAL} library, to support approximate arithmetic on real-valued data. This is particularly effective for biomedical signals and floating-point representations such as ECG readings or sensor vectors.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Encryption Phase:} Data collected by IoT devices is preprocessed at the edge and encrypted using one of four parameter configurations: 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Fast:} 4096 polynomial degree (low security, high performance)
        \item \textbf{Balanced:} 8192 polynomial degree (standard trade-off)
        \item \textbf{Secure:} 16384 polynomial degree (high security, slower execution)
        \item \textbf{High Precision:} 8192 polynomial degree with extended coefficient modulus for increased precision
    \end{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Transmission Phase:} Encrypted ciphertexts are serialized and transmitted to the cloud via secure communication channels. Integrity verification is supported using cryptographic hash functions (MD5, SHA256, and SHA512).
    \item \textbf{Decryption Phase:} Authorized healthcare organizations that possess the correct private keys decrypt the processed data for final analysis and visualization.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Edge Processing Mechanisms}
At the edge, the framework emphasizes rapid encryption, lightweight preprocessing, and scalability:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The edge device benchmarks \textbf{encryption and decryption latency}, \textbf{memory overhead}, and \textbf{parallelization performance}.
    \item Current implementation supports benchmarking multiple encryption contexts across 4 threads, evaluating the trade-off between \textit{security level} and \textit{computation time}.
    \item Planned extensions include homomorphic operations (e.g. addition, multiplication, and polynomial approximation) to enable partial inference at the edge.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cloud Processing Mechanisms}
In the current conceptual design, the cloud layer functions primarily as an \textbf{encrypted data repository and benchmarking environment}. Raw or decrypted data are not stored. Future extensions will enable:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Encrypted Machine Learning Inference:} Applying homomorphic computations directly on encrypted sensor data for model evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Distributed Aggregation:} Combining encrypted data streams from multiple edge nodes to generate privacy-preserving health analytics.
    \item \textbf{Latency Optimization:} Reducing communication time between edge and cloud layers using batched ciphertext transmission and hybrid key management.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
The current stage of this project focuses on the performance and scalability analysis of the TenSEAL CKKS implementation. The following metrics are evaluated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Encryption and Decryption Time:} Average processing time for encrypting and decrypting 300-dimensional vectors under different security levels.
    \item \textbf{Memory Overhead:} Comparison of serialized ciphertext size across encryption contexts.
    \item \textbf{Parallelization Efficiency:} Evaluation of multi-threaded versus sequential execution for FHE workloads.
\end{itemize}

These metrics collectively demonstrate the trade-off between \textit{security}, \textit{efficiency}, and \textit{scalability}, forming the foundation for a fully end-to-end encrypted healthcare data pipeline.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

This section describes the comprehensive experimental framework used to evaluate the performance, scalability, and real-time viability of our FHE-based IoT healthcare pipeline.

\subsection{Hardware and Software Configuration}

\textbf{Development Environment:} All FHE encryption and decryption operations were executed on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7-10700K processor (8 cores, 3.8 GHz base frequency, 5.1 GHz turbo), 32 GB DDR4 RAM, and running Windows 10/11. This configuration represents a typical development and testing environment for healthcare data processing applications.

\textbf{FHE Library and Parameters:} We implemented EdgeSeal-FHE using TenSEAL v0.3.14~\cite{benaissa2021tenseal}, a Python library built on Microsoft SEAL~\cite{sealcrypto} that provides CKKS scheme support. The CKKS (Cheon-Kim-Kim-Song) scheme~\cite{cheon2017homomorphic} enables approximate arithmetic on encrypted floating-point data, making it ideal for medical embeddings. EdgeSeal-FHE's default configuration uses a polynomial modulus degree of 8192 (providing 128-bit security), coefficient modulus bit sizes of [60, 40, 40, 60], and a global scale of $2^{40}$. Galois keys were pre-generated to enable rotation operations on encrypted vectors.

\textbf{Medical Data:} Experiments utilized synthetic 300-dimensional medical embeddings generated by our enhanced autoencoder (detailed in Section~\ref{sec:architecture}). Additionally, we tested with real-world IoT data types including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Vital Signs:} 3-dimensional vectors containing SpO2 (oxygen saturation), heart rate, and body temperature
    \item \textit{ECG Waveforms:} 300--500 sample sequences representing cardiac activity
    \item \textit{Motion Data:} 6--9 dimensional vectors from accelerometer and gyroscope sensors
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Performance Metrics}

Our evaluation framework measures the following key metrics across all experiments:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Encryption Latency:} Time to encrypt a single data sample or batch, measured in milliseconds (ms)
    \item \textbf{Decryption Latency:} Time to decrypt ciphertext back to plaintext
    \item \textbf{Ciphertext Size:} Storage overhead of encrypted data in kilobytes (KB)
    \item \textbf{Throughput:} Number of samples encrypted per second
    \item \textbf{Memory Overhead:} RAM consumption during encryption operations (MB)
    \item \textbf{Reconstruction Accuracy:} Mean Squared Error (MSE) between plaintext and decrypted data
    \item \textbf{Real-time Viability:} Compliance with $<$100ms latency threshold for IoT applications~\cite{bonomi2012fog}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Design}

We conducted four primary categories of experiments:

\textbf{1. Baseline Comparison:} Compared FHE-CKKS encryption against traditional methods including no encryption, AES-256 symmetric encryption~\cite{daemen2002design}, and TLS (Transport Layer Security)~\cite{rescorla2018transport} to quantify the performance overhead of homomorphic encryption.

\textbf{2. Literature Comparison:} Benchmarked EdgeSeal-FHE against 18 state-of-the-art CKKS-based healthcare implementations from 2017--2025~\cite{kim2018logistic, benaissa2021tenseal, lee2022efficient, cheon2017homomorphic} to establish our competitive positioning.

\textbf{3. Configuration Analysis:} Evaluated the impact of CKKS polynomial degree (4096, 8192, 16384) on encryption time, ciphertext size, and security level to guide deployment parameter selection.

\textbf{4. Edge Device Simulation:} Simulated FHE encryption on resource-constrained smartphones across three device tiers (low-end, mid-range, high-end) to assess real-world IoT deployment viability.

All experiments were conducted with 20 trials per configuration to ensure statistical significance. Results report mean values with 95\% confidence intervals. Outliers beyond 2 standard deviations were filtered to account for system noise.

\subsection{Encryption Performance vs. Security Level}

Figure~\ref{fig:fhe-encryption-time} compares encryption latency across different CKKS polynomial degrees for EdgeSeal-FHE versus literature benchmarks. EdgeSeal-FHE achieves \textbf{4.52 ms} encryption time at polynomial degree 8192 (128-bit security), significantly faster than NSysS 2024 on Raspberry Pi~\cite{rahman2024openfhe} (7.1 ms). At the highest security level (polynomial degree 16384, $\sim$256-bit security), EdgeSeal-FHE's encryption time is \textbf{8.60 ms}, providing a practical trade-off between security and performance.

\subsection{Ciphertext Size and Storage Overhead}

Figure~\ref{fig:fhe-ciphertext-size} illustrates the storage overhead of encrypted medical embeddings. For EdgeSeal-FHE's 300-dimensional vectors encrypted with polynomial degree 8192, the ciphertext size is \textbf{326.4 KB}---approximately \textbf{272$\times$} larger than the plaintext (1.2 KB). This overhead is consistent across CKKS implementations~\cite{benaissa2021tenseal}, as the ciphertext size is primarily determined by the polynomial degree and coefficient modulus structure rather than the implementation.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/fhe_ciphertext_size.png}

\label{fig:fhe-ciphertext-size}
\end{figure}

While the storage overhead may appear substantial, it is acceptable for healthcare IoT scenarios because:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Medical embeddings are transmitted infrequently (typically 1--10 samples per minute)
    \item Modern WiFi/4G networks provide sufficient bandwidth (326.4 KB transmits in $\sim$70 ms over 50 Mbps)
    \item The security guarantee of computation on encrypted data justifies the overhead
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Embedding Quality Preservation}

A critical concern for privacy-preserving machine learning is whether encryption degrades data quality. Figure~\ref{fig:fhe-quality-preservation} demonstrates that EdgeSeal-FHE achieves \textbf{99.72\% average reconstruction quality} across all polynomial degrees. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) between plaintext and FHE-encrypted-then-decrypted embeddings ranges from $4.06 \times 10^{-19}$ (poly 4096) to $6.76 \times 10^{-18}$ (poly 16384)---errors so small they are negligible for medical diagnosis applications.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{Graphs/FHE Comparisons/EmbeddingQualityConfigurations.png}
\caption{Embedding quality preservation comparing plaintext vs. FHE-encrypted medical embeddings. EdgeSeal-FHE maintains $>$99.3\% reconstruction quality across all configurations, with average quality loss of only 0.28\%. Error bars show quality degradation (red text indicates loss percentage).}
\label{fig:fhe-quality-preservation}
\end{figure}

This near-perfect preservation is attributed to:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The CKKS scheme's support for approximate arithmetic with configurable precision~\cite{cheon2017homomorphic}
    \item EdgeSeal-FHE's careful selection of coefficient modulus sizes [60, 40, 40, 60] to balance precision and noise growth
    \item The use of normalized embeddings (L2 norm = 1), which reduces numerical instability
\end{enumerate}

The minimal quality loss ($<$0.5\% for poly 8192) ensures that downstream diagnostic models receive high-fidelity encrypted data, preserving the accuracy gains from our medical domain autoencoder.

\subsection{Inference Latency and Computational Complexity}

Figure~\ref{fig:fhe-inference-latency} positions EdgeSeal-FHE's latency within the broader context of encrypted inference frameworks. EdgeSeal-FHE's \textbf{end-to-end pipeline latency of 35.48 ms} (including encryption, transmission, and server-side processing) is orders of magnitude faster than deep learning FHE frameworks like PrivFT~\cite{chen2020privft} (350 ms for query) and Orion~\cite{brutzkus2019low} (60--600 seconds for ResNet inference).

This performance advantage stems from our architectural choice to encrypt \textit{compact embeddings} (300 dimensions) rather than raw high-dimensional data (e.g., ECG waveforms with thousands of samples). By performing dimensionality reduction on the edge device before encryption, we:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reduce encryption time by 10--50$\times$ compared to encrypting raw signals
    \item Minimize ciphertext transmission overhead
    \item Enable real-time processing, which is infeasible for encrypted deep learning inference
\end{itemize}

Counterintuitively, \textbf{low-end devices consume fewer CPU cycles} (41.4B vs. 78.9B) despite slower execution. For battery-powered wearables, this suggests that FHE encryption is \textit{energy-efficient} even on modest hardware.

To contextualize battery impact: a typical smartphone battery is 3000--5000 mAh at 3.7V ($\sim$11--18 Wh). Assuming 5 W power consumption during FHE encryption and 12 samples/sec throughput, continuous encryption would drain the battery in:
\[
\text{Battery Life} = \frac{15 \text{ Wh}}{5 \text{ W}} = 3 \text{ hours (continuous)}
\]

However, medical IoT devices do not encrypt continuously. At 1 sample/minute, encryption consumes only:
\[
\text{Daily Energy} = 5 \text{ W} \times \frac{6.9 \text{ ms}}{60 \text{ s}} \times 1440 \text{ min} = 0.83 \text{ Wh/day}
\]

This represents \textbf{5.5\% of daily battery capacity}---negligible compared to screen, cellular radio, and GPS power consumption. Thus, FHE encryption does not meaningfully impact battery life for typical IoT healthcare use cases.

\subsection{Deployment Recommendations}

Based on our edge device simulation results, we provide the following recommendations for real-world deployment:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Device Compatibility:} Target Android 8.0+ and iOS 12+ devices, which represent 95\%+ of the smartphone market~\cite{statcounter2024}. No specialized hardware (TPU, GPU) is required.

    \item \textbf{Network Optimization:} Deploy edge fog computing nodes~\cite{bonomi2012fog} in hospitals and clinics to reduce network latency from 70 ms (WiFi to cloud) to $<$5 ms (local processing). This could reduce end-to-end latency to 10--15 ms.

    \item \textbf{Data Type Selection:} Prioritize compact embeddings (300-dim) over raw signals (ECG with 5000+ samples) to minimize encryption time and ciphertext size. Our autoencoder achieves this dimensionality reduction with $<$0.5\% information loss.

    \item \textbf{Battery Optimization:} Implement adaptive sampling rates that reduce frequency during low-battery conditions. For non-critical monitoring, switching from 1 sample/sec to 1 sample/minute reduces energy consumption by 60$\times$ while maintaining medical utility.

    \item \textbf{Batching Strategy:} For non-real-time scenarios (e.g., daily health reports), batch 10--100 samples to amortize encryption overhead and improve throughput to 200+ samples/sec.
\end{enumerate}

These optimizations position EdgeSeal-FHE as a practical, deployable solution for privacy-preserving IoT healthcare across diverse device ecosystems and network conditions.
